At Walmart I worked on the service that generates the in-store maps for about 9,000 stores.
It takes a store's raw floor plan, drawn as a CAD-based SVG, and its map data, such as aisle coordinates, department labels, and point-of-interest icons, and turns them into interactive HTML maps.
The iOS and Android apps show those maps in a web view and talk to them through a native bridge for things like dropping a pin, drawing a route, and rotating the map.

That service was still on Node.js 12, which reached end of life in April 2022.
This post is about how we got it to Node 22, and why the path we took was not the obvious one.

Details specific to Walmart's internal systems are left out; what remains is the approach.

## Stuck on both ends

The pressure came from security.
Our dashboard listed critical and major vulnerabilities in the service's npm packages, and the fixes were in newer versions of those packages.
But the newer versions needed a newer Node, and moving to a newer Node needed newer packages.

That is the usual dead end of an old Node service: you cannot upgrade the dependencies without the runtime, or the runtime without the dependencies.
The longer a service sits there, the more both sides move away from it.

## The fork

There was a second, quieter problem.
Our service had started three years earlier as a fork of another team's project.
Since then both sides had kept working.
By the time I looked, we were hundreds of commits behind upstream and hundreds of commits ahead of it, with more than 300 files changed on their side alone.

And upstream had already migrated to Node 22.

That gave us two options:

1. **Upgrade in place.** Bump Node on our fork, then fix whatever broke.
   This repeats work upstream had already done and tested, and leaves our fork even further from it.
2. **Sync upstream first.** Bring in three years of their changes, which include the migration, while keeping every feature we had built on our side.

We chose the second.
It was the bigger job up front, but it was the only option that ended with us closer to upstream instead of further away, and it reused a migration someone had already debugged.

## How the sync went

The rule was simple: nothing comes in unread, and nothing of ours goes away by accident.

- **Merge everything, then review every file.** I merged all three years of upstream commits and went through each changed file by hand rather than trusting the merge to know which side was right.
- **Keep a ledger of decisions.** For every change, I wrote down whether we took it or left it, and why.
  With hundreds of conflicts, that record is what made the result reviewable.
- **Ask the people who wrote it.** When upstream logic was unclear, I asked the upstream engineers instead of guessing.
- **Take only what we needed.** Not every upstream change belonged in our product.
  We took what the Node 22 migration needed, plus the performance and security changes, and left the rest.
- **Protect our own features.** Our side had three years of work that upstream did not have, so every resolved conflict was checked against the map features our apps depend on.

Then came verification.
Map generation had to keep producing the same maps with every feature intact, because thousands of stores and several apps depended on its output.

## What came out of it

- **It fit in one sprint.**
- **The dependencies could finally move.** Once we were on Node 22, we upgraded the packages and cleared every critical and major vulnerability on the dashboard.
- **Map generation got 30 to 90% faster**, depending on the size of the store.

The speedup was not the goal.
It came with what the sync brought in: upstream's performance changes, on a runtime ten major versions newer.
Upgrading Node alone on our fork would have fixed the security problem and left those changes behind.

## What I took away

- **A fork's debt compounds quietly.** Every month without a sync makes the next one more expensive, and nobody notices until something forces it.
  A small regular sync is far cheaper than a three-year one.
- **Look for the work that is already done.** The fastest way through a migration was someone else's finished migration.
- **Write decisions down while you make them.** The ledger turned hundreds of judgment calls into something a reviewer could follow.
- **Scope is part of the job.** Taking everything upstream had would have been easier to merge and harder to ship.
  Taking only what we needed is what kept it to one sprint.
